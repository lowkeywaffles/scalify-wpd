What this book is, and what it isn't

You can edit. Nobody is paying you yet, or only a friend has. This book is a 30-day plan to change that, by selling one specific thing to one specific kind of buyer: short promo and ad videos for small businesses.

It is a field guide, so it is built to be used, not just read. Every chapter ends with a task. The scripts are written out word for word. The kit that comes with it has a tracker, the scripts as a text file, a client brief and a simple agreement you can adapt.

Honest limits

This book cannot promise you clients or income. Nobody can. What it gives you is a clear method, a daily workload, and a way to measure what is happening so you can fix the right thing.

The numbers in it (prices, reply rates) are ranges from public sources and my own experience, and yours will differ. The agreement template is a plain-language starting point, not legal advice. Tax and payment rules depend on your country, so check yours.

The whole method in six lines
  1. Choose one type of business.
  2. Make three sample ads for businesses like them.
  3. Offer one clear package at one clear price.
  4. Contact 5 to 10 of those businesses every day, with a message about their business.
  5. Take a deposit, deliver fast, ask for a testimonial.
  6. Use that testimonial to win the next two.

Chapter 1 · Day 1

Why good editors stay at zero

Almost nobody fails at this because their edits are bad. They fail for five boring reasons, and every one of them can be fixed this month.

1. They sell "video editing"

A bakery owner does not wake up wanting video editing. She wants more people to order cakes this weekend. "I'm a video editor, do you need any editing?" asks her to work out what you could do for her. "I make 20-second ads for bakeries that show the cake being made, sized for Instagram and TikTok" does that work for her.

2. Their portfolio shows the wrong thing

Anime edits, gaming montages and velocity edits prove you know the software. They don't prove you can sell a haircut. A business owner needs to see something that looks like what she would get.

3. They wait to be found

Posting a profile on a marketplace and waiting puts you in a queue with thousands of editors, many with reviews you don't have yet. Beginners report very low reply rates there. One estimate from a freelancer mentor is about 1 reply in 15 for specific proposals and 1 in 100 for copy-paste ones. Direct contact with businesses you chose is slower per message and much better per reply.

4. They send ten messages and stop

Outreach is a numbers game with a quality rule. Ten messages tells you nothing. A hundred good ones tells you a lot. This plan has you send about 100 over two and a half weeks.

5. They don't know what to say when someone answers "how much?"

So they panic, say a tiny number, or disappear. Chapter 4 fixes the price. Chapter 8 gives you the words.

Why businesses, not YouTubers

Most beginner advice says to chase YouTubers. That works for some people, and it is crowded. Small businesses are a different pond:

  • A video that brings in customers pays for itself, so price is easier to justify.
  • Most small businesses post weak video or none, and they know it.
  • There are far more small businesses than full-time creators, in every city and online.
  • Three samples are enough to start. You don't need to match a famous creator's style.
Task for today

Write down, honestly, which of the five reasons is yours. Most people have two. You'll check back on day 30.

Chapter 2 · Day 1–2

Pick a lane

For the next 30 days you make promo videos for one type of business. Not forever. For 30 days. A lane makes every later step easier: your samples match, your message is specific, and one happy client can refer you to others like them.

What a good lane looks like

  • They already post, badly. A business with an active Instagram or TikTok full of blurry clips has already decided video matters. You're not convincing them, you're upgrading them.
  • One new customer is worth real money. A dentist, gym, salon, tutor or real-estate agent earns a lot more from one new customer than your video costs.
  • The product looks good on camera. Food, hair, fitness, cars, interiors, events, fashion.
  • You can reach the owner. The person who reads the DMs is the person who decides.
  • You understand it a little. If you lift weights, gyms are easier for you than law firms.

Where the footage comes from

You're an editor, not a camera crew. There are three honest ways to work:

  1. They film on a phone, you direct. You send a shot list (chapter 10 has one). Most owners can film eight 5-second clips.
  2. You use what they already posted. Their existing clips, photos and reviews, re-cut properly.
  3. No footage at all. Text, photos, screen recordings and motion graphics. Ideal for apps, online stores and services.

Local clients can also be filmed by you on your phone. That's a bonus, not a requirement.

Chapter 3 · Day 3–7

Build a 3-piece portfolio with no clients

You don't need permission to make a sample. You need three short videos that look like what your lane would buy. These are called spec pieces, and every agency started with them.

The three pieces

PieceWhat it isLength
1. The offer adOne clear offer: "First class free", "20% off this week", "Order by Friday".15–20 s
2. The "why us" adThree reasons to choose this business, shown not told.20–30 s
3. The before/afterA real weak video from a business in your lane, re-edited by you, shown side by side.15–25 s

Piece 3 does the heaviest lifting. It shows the gap between what they have and what you make in a few seconds, with no explaining.

Where to get material, legally

  • Free stock footage with a licence that allows commercial use (for example Pexels, Pixabay and Mixkit). Read each site's licence page before you use a clip.
  • Your own phone. Film your own gym session, a coffee being made, a friend's shop with their OK.
  • An invented brand. Make up "Northside Barbers". An invented brand has no owner to complain.
  • Licensed music and sounds only. Use libraries that allow commercial use. Chart music can get a client's ad muted or taken down.
Be honest about spec work

Label samples as "concept" or "spec ad". Never imply a business hired you when it didn't, and don't use a real company's logo as if it were a client. For the before/after, either use a business that agreed, or blur the name and say "re-edit of a real post, shown for comparison". It protects you, and owners respect it.

The 8-part ad that works for almost any business

  1. Hook (0–2 s). The most striking shot or a short line: "Your Friday plans, sorted."
  2. Problem or desire (2–5 s). What the customer wants.
  3. The product in action (5–12 s). Fast cuts, 1 to 1.5 seconds each.
  4. Proof (12–16 s). A review quote, a number, a before/after.
  5. Offer (16–19 s). One offer, one line.
  6. Call to action (last 2 s). "Book in bio", "Order on WhatsApp", with the handle on screen.
  7. Captions throughout. Many people watch on mute.
  8. Sound. A music bed and 4 to 8 sound effects on the cuts.

Quality bar before you show anyone

  • Vertical 9:16, 1080×1920, sharp.
  • Text is inside the safe area (not under the app's buttons or caption).
  • Readable on a phone at arm's length. Nothing smaller than a caption.
  • Something changes on screen at least every 2 seconds.
  • The offer and how to act on it are clear with the sound off.
  • No spelling mistakes. Read every word aloud.
  • Shown to one person outside editing who can say what's being sold after one watch.

Where your portfolio lives

Keep it simple: one link that opens fast on a phone. A pinned post or highlight on an Instagram or TikTok account used only for your work is enough. A free one-page site or a shared folder also works. Put the before/after first, then the other two, then one line on what you do and how to reach you.

Task (5 days)

Day 3: gather material and write three 6-line scripts. Days 4–6: one piece per day. Day 7: fix, export, set up the one link, and get one outside opinion.

Chapter 4 · Day 8

Your offer and your prices

An offer is a sentence a busy owner can say yes to. It names what they get, how fast, and for how much.

Write your offer sentence

I make [number] short promo videos for [type of business] that [result they want], delivered in [days] days, for [price].

Example: "I make 3 short promo videos for gyms that show off your space and your offer, delivered in 5 days, for $150."

Three packages

Offer three choices. Most people pick the middle one, and three options change the question from "yes or no?" to "which one?".

StarterStandardMonthly
What1 ad, 15–30 s3 ads from one brief8 videos a month
IncludesCaptions, music, 1 revision roundSame, plus 3 different hooksSame, plus a monthly plan call
Delivery3 days5 days2 a week
Beginner price range$40–$80$100–$200$250–$500

Ranges reflect public 2026 figures for short-form work (commonly $20–$75 per clip from freelancers, more with motion graphics) and my own jobs at $50–$200. Local prices vary a lot: adjust to what businesses near you can pay.

Find your own floor price

Never guess. Work it out once:

Hours for one ad (be honest, include revisions) = ___ Minimum you accept per hour = ___ Floor price = hours × hourly minimum = ___ Add 20% for messages, exports, fixes = ___ ← never go below this

If a starter ad takes you 4 hours and your minimum is $10 an hour, your floor is $40 and your price with the buffer is $48. Round to $50.

Rules that protect you

  • First three clients: a launch price, said out loud. "My normal price is $150. I'm taking three launch clients at $100 in exchange for a testimonial." A discount with a reason and a limit is honest. A fake "normal price" is not, so only say it if you will really charge it next.
  • Never free for a full project. A short free sample to open a conversation is fine (chapter 7). A whole job for "exposure" is not.
  • One revision round is included. Extra rounds cost a set fee. Say it before you start.
  • 50% before you start. Always. Chapter 9.
  • Raise your price after every third client by 20 to 30 percent until people start saying no about a third of the time.
Task

Write your offer sentence, your three packages and your floor price. Put them in a note on your phone. You will paste from it many times.

Chapter 5 · Day 9–10

Build a list of 50 businesses

Good outreach starts before the message. Fifty carefully chosen businesses beat five hundred random ones.

Where to find them

  • Instagram and TikTok search. Search your lane plus a city: "barber Nairobi", "gym Dubai". Then open the accounts they follow and the "suggested" row. One good account leads to twenty.
  • Google Maps. Search the lane in a city. Businesses with many reviews and a weak social page are ideal: customers love them and their video doesn't show it.
  • Ad libraries. Meta and TikTok both have public ad libraries. A business already paying for ads with weak creative has a budget and a need.
  • Hashtags and location tags for your lane.
  • Your own life. The places you already buy from.

Qualify each one in 60 seconds

SignalGood signSkip if
ActivityPosted in the last 2 weeksDead for months
Video qualityPosts video, but shaky, no captions, slow startAlready polished (they have an editor)
SizeRoughly 1k–50k followers, or a busy local shopBig chain with a marketing department

Money signalRuns ads, has staff, premium prices, many reviewsClearly a hobby

Write down one true observation per business

This is the step almost everyone skips, and it's the reason your message will get read. For each business, note one specific thing you noticed:

  • "Your transformation post from last Tuesday got 3× your usual likes, but it's a photo. That would work even better as a 15-second video."
  • "Your reel of the new machine starts with 4 seconds of walking before anything happens."
  • "You have 212 five-star reviews on Maps and none of them appear in your videos."

Put it in the tracker's "Observation" column. If you can't find anything specific to say, they aren't a good fit, so skip them.

Task (2 days)

Fill 50 rows of the tracker: name, link, where you'll contact them, who decides, one observation. About 25 a day, around 2 hours each day.

Chapter 6 · Day 11

Warm outreach: the 20-name list

Your first client is more likely to come from someone who already knows you than from a stranger. Half my own clients came this way. It feels awkward for about ten minutes.

Make the list

Write 20 names. Not people who need a video: people who know people. Family, classmates, former teachers, your barber, the café you sit in, anyone who runs or works at a business, anyone with a wide circle.

The message

You are not asking them to buy. You're asking who they know. That makes it easy to help you.

Hey [name], quick one. I've started making short promo videos for [type of business]. Here are 3 I made: [link] Do you know anyone who runs a [type of business] who might want something like this? I'm taking 3 launch clients at a lower price this month. No worries if nobody comes to mind!

If they say "I might need one myself"

That would be great. What's the main thing you want more of right now, bookings, orders, or walk-ins? I'll suggest the video that fits and send you the price.
Do it properly
  • Send each one personally, with their name. No group messages.
  • Send all 20 on the same day so you can't talk yourself out of it.
  • Thank everyone who replies, whatever they say. Ask again in two months with new work.
Task

20 names, 20 messages, today. Log them in the tracker with the channel "Warm".

Chapter 7 · Day 12–24

Cold outreach that gets replies

Ten messages a day, five days a week, for two and a half weeks. Each one is about them, short, and easy to answer.

The four-line message

  1. Their name and one true observation (from your tracker).
  2. One idea for a video that fits what you saw.
  3. Proof: your link.
  4. A small question that takes one word to answer.
Hi [name], your [specific post] caught my eye, [the true observation]. I make short promo videos for [lane]. I think a 15-second version showing [idea] would do well for you. Examples of my work: [link] Want me to put together a quick concept for [business name]? No cost for the concept.

What makes this different from spam

  • It could only have been sent to this one business.
  • It's under 70 words.
  • No price, no life story, no "I hope this finds you well".
  • The question is easy: yes or no.

The free sample, used carefully

Offering a short free sample is the most recommended tactic in this field, and it does open doors. It can also eat your month. My rules:

  • Offer the sample after they reply with interest, not in bulk to everyone.
  • Cap it: one 10–15 second cut, one hour of your time, made from their existing posts.
  • Put a small "sample" watermark in a corner until they pay.
  • Send it with your packages attached, so the next step is obvious.
  • At most three free samples a week.

Follow up, because most replies come from follow-ups

WhenWhat you send
Day 0The four-line message
Day 3"Hi [name], just floating this up in case it got buried. Happy to send a quick concept for [business]."
Day 7Something useful with no ask: "Saw this ad from a [lane] in another city and thought of you: [what they did well]. This format would suit your [product]."
Day 14"I'll stop here so I'm not crowding your inbox. If video becomes a priority, my work is at [link]. Wishing you a great month."

Then stop. Four touches is polite persistence. More is pestering.

Which channel

  • Instagram or TikTok DM for small, owner-run businesses. Like or comment on a post first, sincerely, a day before.
  • Email for businesses that list one. Subject line: "Video idea for [business name]".
  • WhatsApp or Telegram only if they publish that number for business enquiries.
  • In person for local shops. Show the samples on your phone. It works better than anything online.
Stay inside the rules
  • New accounts that send many identical DMs get restricted. Keep to about 10–15 a day, each written for that business.
  • Only contact business accounts and published business addresses. If someone says no or stop, stop, and mark it in the tracker.
  • Laws on marketing messages differ by country. A short, personal, relevant message to a business about its business, with an easy way to say no, is the safe standard.

What to expect, honestly

I could not find trustworthy published reply rates for this kind of outreach. Anyone quoting "90% replies" is selling something. So measure your own. As a rough guide for planning: if 100 well-aimed messages bring 8 to 15 replies, 3 to 6 real conversations and 1 to 3 clients, the system is working. If your numbers are far below that after 50 messages, chapter 12 shows what to change.

Daily task (13 working days)

10 new messages + all follow-ups due today. Log every one. About 60–90 minutes.

Chapter 8 · As replies come

From reply to "yes"

A reply is not a sale. It's an invitation to understand what they want. The person who asks better questions wins the job.

Reply within the hour if you can

Interest fades fast. Keep notifications on during outreach weeks.

Five questions before any price

Ask these in chat or on a 10-minute call. Write the answers down: they become your brief.

  1. "What do you most want more of right now: bookings, orders, walk-ins, followers?"
  2. "Is there an offer or event coming up that we should build this around?"
  3. "Who's your best kind of customer?"
  4. "What video have you posted that did well, and one that flopped?"
  5. "Where will this run: just your page, or as a paid ad too?"

Then send a one-screen proposal

Thanks [name]. Here's what I'd suggest for [business]: GOAL: more [bookings] for [offer] WHAT: 3 vertical ads (15–20 s), captions and music included, each with a different opening YOU SEND: 8 short phone clips (I'll send a shot list) + logo TIMING: first drafts 5 days after I get the clips REVISIONS: one round included PRICE: $150 (launch price, 3 spots). 50% to start, 50% on delivery. Shall I send the shot list and payment details?

Never promise sales

You control the quality and the delivery date. You don't control their product, their prices or the algorithm. Promise what you control. Clients trust you more for it.

Chapter 9 · Before any work

Deposit, agreement, getting paid

The two rules that prevent almost every beginner horror story: write it down, and get half first.

The one-page agreement

You don't need a lawyer's contract for a $100 job. You need both sides to agree, in writing, on eight things. A message they reply "agreed" to is enough for small jobs. The kit has a template.

  1. Who the two parties are.
  2. What you'll deliver: number, length, format.
  3. What they must send you and by when.
  4. Delivery date, counted from when you receive their material.
  5. Revisions: one round included, then a fee per extra round.
  6. Price and payment: 50% to start, 50% before final files.
  7. Rights: they can use the videos for their business once paid in full; you can show the work in your portfolio.
  8. If either side cancels: the deposit covers work already done.

Getting paid

  • Local clients: whatever they already use daily, such as bank transfer or mobile money. Ask for the transaction reference.
  • Clients abroad: options include international payment services, freelancer payout services, or a platform that handles checkout for you. Which ones work depends on your country and theirs, and the rules change. Check what is available and legal where you live before you quote a foreign client, and factor the fees into your price.
  • Deliver finals after the second payment. Send a watermarked preview for approval, then the clean files once paid.
  • Keep records of every payment and message. You may owe tax on this income. Ask someone who knows your country's rules.
Red flags: walk away
  • Refuses any deposit.
  • "Do this one cheap and there'll be lots more work."
  • Wants to overpay and have you send money back.
  • Asks you to use pirated footage or music, or to copy a competitor's ad exactly.
  • Is rude before paying. It gets worse after.

Chapter 10 · Each job

Deliver so they come back

A repeat client costs you zero outreach. Good delivery is your cheapest marketing.

The shot list you send them

Please film these on your phone, upright (vertical), 5–8 seconds each, in good light. Don't worry about sound. 1. The front of the business from outside 2. Wide shot of the inside 3. Your product/service close up, in action (3 clips) 4. A happy customer or the finished result 5. You or your team, smiling at camera 6. Anything that makes you different Also send: your logo, your offer in one sentence, and 1–2 customer reviews I can quote.

Your workflow

  1. Day 0: deposit in, shot list sent, delivery date confirmed in writing.
  2. Material arrives: check it the same day. Ask for re-shoots now, not later.
  3. Script first: six lines of on-screen text. Send it for a quick OK before you edit. This avoids most revisions.
  4. Edit using the 8-part structure from chapter 3.
  5. Preview: send watermarked drafts one day early.
  6. One revision round: ask for all changes in one message.
  7. Final payment, then finals: clean files, plus a version without captions and the thumbnail frame.
  8. One week later: ask how it performed.

Small things clients remember

  • You deliver a day early.
  • File names make sense: BusinessName_Offer_v1_9x16.mp4.
  • You include a suggested caption and three hashtags.
  • You tell them the best version to post first, and why.
  • You never go quiet. If you're late, you say so before the deadline.
When revisions don't stop

"Happy to make those. That's beyond the round we agreed, so it would be $15 for this extra round. Shall I go ahead?" Calm, friendly, in writing. Most clients say yes or suddenly decide the video is fine.

Chapter 11 · Day 25–30

Turn one client into three

Your first happy client is worth more than the fee. They are proof, a referral source and possibly a monthly customer.

1. Ask for a testimonial the moment they're pleased

So glad you like them! Could you write 2–3 sentences I can share: what you needed, what it was like working with me, and what you think of the result? A short voice note works too and I'll type it up for your OK.

Use their words exactly, with their name and business, and only with permission. Never write a testimonial yourself or tidy it into something they didn't say.

2. Ask for one introduction

One more thing: do you know one other business owner who could use videos like these? If you introduce us and they book, your next video is 20% off.

3. Offer the monthly plan when results show

The offer ad got [their number] views, nice. Businesses that post new video every week tend to keep that going. I can do 8 videos a month for $300, two delivered each week, so you never have to think about it. Want to try one month?

Only quote numbers they gave you or that you can both see on the post.

4. Put the proof to work

  • Add the real project to the top of your portfolio, above the spec pieces.
  • Post a before/after of the job (with permission).
  • Add one line to your outreach: "I recently made [X] for [business], here's the result: [link]." Replies improve noticeably once you have a real client to name.
  • Go back to everyone who said "not now" with the new work.

5. Raise your price

After three paid jobs, the launch price ends, as you said it would. Move up 20 to 30 percent.
