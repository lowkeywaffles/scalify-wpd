What this book is, and what it isn't

You can edit. Nobody is paying you yet, or only a friend has. This book is a 30-day plan to change that, by selling one specific thing to one specific kind of buyer: short promo and ad videos for small businesses.

It is a field guide, so it is built to be used, not just read. Every chapter ends with a task. The scripts are written out word for word. The kit in the same download has a working outreach tracker, a package-pricing calculator and editable client documents (see the Appendix for links).

Three clients is the goal, not a promise. Some readers will get there in 30 days, some will take longer, and some will learn that their first lane was the wrong one. All three outcomes are useful if you track what happens.

Honest limits

This book cannot promise you clients or income. Nobody can. What it gives you is a clear method, a daily workload, and a way to measure what is happening so you can fix the right thing.

Numbers in it are either ranges from public sources (listed at the back), or my own experience, and each is labelled. Much of the advice is judgement, not research: where I say "usually" or "in my experience", that's what it means. The templates are plain-language starting points, not legal advice. Marketing, copyright, tax and payment rules depend on your country, so check yours.

Chapter 1 · Day 1

Why good editors stay at zero

Editing skill matters: weak pacing, unreadable text, bad audio or an unclear offer will lose you clients. But many capable editors still stay at zero, and in my experience it's usually for one of five boring reasons. Each can be worked on this month.

1. They sell "video editing"

A bakery owner does not wake up wanting video editing. She wants more people to order cakes this weekend. "I'm a video editor, do you need any editing?" asks her to work out what you could do for her. "I make 20-second ads for bakeries that show the cake being made, sized for Instagram and TikTok" does that work for her.

2. Their portfolio shows the wrong thing

Anime edits, gaming montages and velocity edits prove you know the software. They don't prove you can sell a haircut. A business owner needs to see something that looks like what she would get.

3. They wait to be found

Posting a profile on a marketplace and waiting puts you in a queue with many editors who already have reviews. That doesn't mean marketplaces never work; some editors do well there. But beginners commonly report few replies. One freelancer mentor estimates about 1 reply per 15 specific proposals and 1 per 100 copy-paste ones (an estimate, not a study). Contacting businesses you chose takes longer per message, and you control who sees your work.

4. They send ten messages and stop

Ten messages tells you very little. A hundred well-aimed ones starts to show a pattern. This plan has you send 110 first messages over about two and a half weeks, plus follow-ups.

5. They don't know what to say when someone answers "how much?"

So they panic, say a tiny number, or disappear. Chapter 4 fixes the price. Chapter 8 gives you the words.

Why businesses, not YouTubers

A lot of beginner advice says to chase YouTubers. That works for some people, and it is crowded. Small businesses are a different pond:

  • If a video brings in customers, it can pay for itself, which makes price easier to discuss.
  • Many small businesses post little video, or video that clearly could be better. Look at 20 in your area and judge for yourself.
  • There are businesses in every city and online, and each one is a possible buyer.
  • You can start with three samples. You don't need to match a famous creator's style.
Task for today

Write down, honestly, which of the five reasons apply to you, and whether your editing itself needs work (ask one honest friend). You'll check back on day 30.

Chapter 2 · Day 1–2

Pick a lane

For the next 30 days you make promo videos for one type of business. Not forever. For 30 days. A lane makes later steps easier: your samples match, your message is specific, and a happy client may know others like them.

What a good lane looks like

  • They already post, but it could be better. A business with an active Instagram or TikTok has already decided video matters. You're offering an upgrade, not a new idea.
  • One new customer is worth real money. For a dentist, gym, salon, tutor or real-estate agent, one or two new customers can be worth more than your video costs.
  • The product looks good on camera. Food, hair, fitness, cars, interiors, events, fashion.
  • You can reach the owner. The person who reads the DMs is the person who decides.
  • You understand it a little. If you lift weights, gyms are easier for you than law firms.

Where the footage comes from

You're an editor, not a camera crew. There are three honest ways to work:

  1. They film on a phone, you direct. You send a shot list (chapter 10 has one). Most owners can film eight 5-second clips.
  2. You use what they already posted. Their existing clips, photos and reviews, re-cut properly.
  3. No footage at all. Text, photos, screen recordings and motion graphics. Ideal for apps, online stores and services.

For local clients you can also film on your phone. That's a bonus, not a requirement. Whoever films, the client should confirm they have the right to use what they send (people, music, logos), which the agreement in the kit covers.

Chapter 3 · Day 3–7

Build a 3-piece portfolio with no clients

You don't need a client to make a sample. You need three short videos that look like what your lane would buy, built only from material you're allowed to use. These are called spec pieces.

The three pieces

PieceWhat it isLength
1. The offer adOne clear offer: "First class free", "20% off this week", "Order by Friday".15–20 s
2. The "why us" adThree reasons to choose this business, shown not told.20–30 s
3. The before/afterA business's existing video, re-edited by you with their permission, shown side by side. If you have no permission yet, use your own raw footage as the "before".15–25 s

Piece 3 usually does the most work, because it shows the difference in a few seconds. Getting permission is easier than it sounds and doubles as outreach: "Could I re-edit one of your posts as a free portfolio piece? You can use the result." A yes gives you a sample and a warm contact.

Where to get material, legally

Every clip, photo, song, font and logo in a sample has an owner. Seeing it online does not mean you may reuse or adapt it, and owning a copy of a photo is not the same as having the right to reproduce it. Use only these:

  • Footage you shot yourself. Your own gym session, a coffee you made, a friend's shop with their written OK (and OK from anyone recognisable in it).
  • Stock with a licence that allows commercial use and editing (for example Pexels, Pixabay, Mixkit). Read each site's licence page, and keep a note of where every clip came from.
  • A real business's material, with their permission in writing. A message saying "yes, go ahead" is enough for a portfolio piece.
  • Music and sound effects from libraries that allow commercial use. Chart music can get an ad muted or removed.

An invented brand ("Northside Barbers") is useful for the offer and "why us" ads. It avoids implying a client relationship, but you still need rights to every asset in it.

Be honest about spec work

Label samples as "concept" or "spec ad". Never imply a business hired you when it didn't, and don't use a real company's logo as if it were a client. A label explains what the piece is; it does not give you rights to the material. Blurring a name doesn't either. If you're unsure whether you may use something, don't.

A starting structure (adapt the pace to the brand)

Most short promos contain these parts. The order and timing change with the business: an energetic gym ad and a calm luxury salon ad should not feel the same. The timings below fit a 20-second ad; see the three examples on the next page for other paces.

  1. Hook (0–2 s). The most striking shot or a short line: "Your Friday plans, sorted."
  2. Desire (2–4 s). What the customer wants.
  3. The product in action (4–11 s). Cut length depends on the mood: quick for energy, longer for calm.
  4. Proof (11–14 s). A real review quote (with permission), a before/after.
  5. Offer (14–17 s). One offer, one line.
  6. Call to action (17–20 s). "Book in bio", "Order on WhatsApp", with the handle on screen.

Captions help because many people watch with the sound off. Sound design (music, effects) should match the mood: some ads use many effects, a calm ad may use almost none.

Quality bar before you show anyone

  • Vertical 9:16, 1080×1920, sharp.
  • Text is inside the safe area (not under the app's buttons or caption).
  • Readable on a phone at arm's length. Nothing smaller than a caption.
  • The pace fits the brand, and nothing sits still long enough to feel stuck.
  • The offer and how to act on it are clear with the sound off.
  • No spelling mistakes. Read every word aloud.
  • Audio is clean and not clipping; music doesn't drown speech.
  • Every asset is yours, licensed or permitted, and noted in a sources list.
  • Shown to one person outside editing who can say what's being sold after one watch.

Keep it simple: one link that opens fast on a phone. A pinned post or highlight on an Instagram or TikTok account used only for your work is enough. A free one-page site or a shared folder also works. Put your strongest piece first (often the before/after), then the other two, then one line on what you do and how to reach you. Chapter 4 · Day 8

Your offer and your prices

An offer is a sentence a busy owner can say yes to. It names what they get, how fast, and for how much.

Write your offer sentence

I make [number] short promo videos for [type of business] that [result they want], delivered in [days] days, for [price].

Example: "I make 3 short promo videos for gyms that show off your space and your offer, delivered in 5 days, for $150."

Three packages

Offering a few clearly different choices lets the client pick what fits, instead of just saying yes or no. Be precise about what each includes, because "3 ads" can mean very different amounts of work.

StarterStandardMonthly batch
What1 ad, 15–30 s1 core ad + 2 alternate openings8 short videos from one batch of footage, using one template
Included9:16 export with captions, licensed music, 1 revision roundSame. The middle and end are shared; only the first 2–3 s changeSame, plus a 20-minute planning call each month
Not includedFilming, extra revision rounds, other sizes (1:1, 16:9), versions without captions, thumbnails, paid stock or music. Price each extra separately.

Example price$50$90$320

Three separate concepts (different ideas, different footage) are three times the work of one ad. Price them as three Starters, perhaps with a small discount. Don't give them away as a "Standard".

The example prices fit the floor-price example below. Public 2026 guides put freelance short-form work commonly at about $20–$75 per clip, more with motion graphics; my own jobs have been $50–$200. Local prices vary a lot.

Find your own floor price

Never guess. Work it out once:

Hours for one ad (be honest, include revisions) = ___ Minimum you accept per hour = ___ Floor price = hours × hourly minimum = ___ Add 20% for messages, exports, fixes = ___ ← never go below this

If a Starter ad takes you 4 hours and your minimum is $10 an hour, your floor is $40, or $48 with the buffer. Round to $50.

Check every package, not just one ad. The Standard adds two openings: maybe 3 more hours, so 7 × $10 × 1.2 = $84. I'd quote $90. The monthly batch only works if batching really saves time. If, with one template and one batch of footage, each video takes you 2.5 hours plus a 1-hour call, that's 21 hours: 21 × $10 × 1.2 = $252 before costs. $320 leaves room for music, fees and slow weeks. If each video still takes you 4 hours, the batch would need to be about $400 or more. The pricing calculator in the kit does this maths for you.

Rules that protect you

  • First three clients: a launch price, said out loud. "This package will be $110. For my first three clients it's $90, in exchange for a testimonial." A discount with a reason and a limit is honest. A fake "normal price" is not, so only say it if you will really charge it next. The launch price must still be at or above your floor price. (The example prices in the table above are launch prices.)
  • Never free for a full project. A short free sample to open a conversation is fine (chapter 7). A whole job for "exposure" is not.
  • One revision round is included. Extra rounds cost a set fee. Say it before you start.
  • 50% before you start. Always. Chapter 9.
  • Raise prices for a reason, not a rule. Good reasons: you're busy enough that you're turning work down, your real hours per job are higher than you priced, or you have results and testimonials to show. When people say no, find out why before cutting the price: it may be the wrong lane or an unclear offer.
Task

Write your offer sentence, your three packages and your floor price. Put them in a note on your phone. You will paste from it many times.

Chapter 5 · Day 9–10

Build a list of 70 businesses

Good outreach starts before the message. Seventy carefully chosen businesses beat five hundred random ones. You'll add 40 more on day 17, for 110 in total.

Where to find them

  • Instagram and TikTok search. Search your lane plus a city: "barber Nairobi", "gym Dubai". Then open the accounts they follow and the "suggested" row. One good account leads to twenty.
  • Google Maps. Search the lane in a city. Businesses with many reviews and a weak social page are ideal: customers love them and their video doesn't show it.
  • Ad libraries. Meta and TikTok both have public ad libraries. A business already paying for ads with weak creative has a budget and a need.
  • Hashtags and location tags for your lane.
  • Your own life. The places you already buy from.

Qualify each one in 60 seconds

SizeRoughly 1k–50k followers, or a busy local shopBig chain with a marketing department

Write down one true observation per business

This is the step almost everyone skips, and it's the reason your message will get read. For each business, note one specific thing you noticed:

  • "Your transformation post from last Tuesday got 3× your usual likes, but it's a photo. That would work even better as a 15-second video."
  • "Your reel of the new machine starts with 4 seconds of walking before anything happens."
  • "You have 212 five-star reviews on Maps and none of them appear in your videos."

Put it in the tracker's "Observation" column. If you can't find anything specific and true to say, skip them for now.

Task (2 days)

Fill 70 rows of the tracker: name, link, channel, who decides, one observation. About 35 a day. Expect 2 to 3 hours a day at first; you get faster.

Chapter 6 · Day 11

Warm outreach: the 20-name list

People who already know you are often the easiest first step, because they trust you. Some of my own clients came this way. It feels awkward for about ten minutes.

Make the list

Write 20 names. Not people who need a video: people who know people. Family, classmates, former teachers, your barber, the café you sit in, anyone who runs or works at a business, anyone with a wide circle.

The message

You are not asking them to buy. You're asking who they know. That makes it easy to help you.

Hey [name], quick one. I've started making short promo videos for [type of business]. Here are 3 I made: [link] Do you know anyone who runs a [type of business] who might want something like this? I'm taking 3 launch clients at a lower price this month. No worries if nobody comes to mind!

If they say "I might need one myself"

That would be great. What's the main thing you want more of right now, bookings, orders, or walk-ins? I'll suggest the video that fits and send you the price.
Do it properly
  • Send each one personally, with their name. No group messages.
  • Send all 20 on the same day so you can't talk yourself out of it.
  • Thank everyone who replies, whatever they say. Ask again in two months with new work.
Task

20 names, 20 messages, today. Log them in the tracker with the channel "Warm".

Chapter 7 · Day 12–24

Cold outreach that gets replies

About 10 first messages on each outreach day (the calendar in chapter 12 has 11 of them), plus the follow-ups that fall due. Each one is about them, short, and easy to answer.

The four-line message

  1. Their name and one true observation (from your tracker).
  2. One idea for a video that fits what you saw.
  3. Proof: your link.
  4. A small question that takes one word to answer.
Hi [name], your [specific post] caught my eye, [the true observation]. I make short promo videos for [lane]. I think a 15-second version showing [idea] would do well for you. Examples of my work: [link] Want me to put together a quick concept for [business name]? No cost for the concept.

What makes this different from spam

  • It could only have been sent to this one business.
  • It's under 70 words.
  • No price, no life story, no "I hope this finds you well".
  • The question is easy: yes or no.

The free sample, used carefully

Offering a short free sample is the most recommended tactic in this field, and it does open doors. It can also eat your month. My rules:

  • Offer the sample after they reply with interest, not in bulk to everyone.
  • Cap it: one 10–15 second cut, one hour of your time, made from their existing posts.
  • Put a small "sample" watermark in a corner until they pay.
  • Send it with your packages attached, so the next step is obvious.
  • At most three free samples a week.

Follow up: many replies come after a reminder

Busy owners miss messages. A polite reminder is normal. The tracker in the kit works out these dates for you.

WhenWhat you send
Day 0The four-line message
Day 3"Hi [name], just floating this up in case it got buried. Happy to send a quick concept for [business]."

Then stop. Four touches is polite persistence. More is pestering.

Which channel

  • Instagram or TikTok DM for small, owner-run businesses. Like or comment on a post first, sincerely, a day before.
  • Email for businesses that list one. Subject line: "Video idea for [business name]".
  • WhatsApp or Telegram only if they publish that number for business enquiries.
  • In person for local shops, at a quiet time of day. Showing samples on your phone face to face can start a conversation more easily than a message.
Check the rules before you send

Personalising a message doesn't make it automatically legal everywhere. The rules depend on where you are, where they are, who they are and which channel you use. Before your first send, spend 15 minutes checking:

  • Their country's marketing rules. For example, UK rules treat companies differently from sole traders and some partnerships, count social-media DMs as electronic mail, and don't treat published contact details as permission to market (see the ICO link in the sources). In the US, commercial email has CAN-SPAM rules (FTC link in the sources).
  • The platform's terms. Platforms don't publish a "safe" number of DMs. Write each message by hand, go slowly, and stop if you get warnings.
  • Their wishes. If someone says no or stop, stop, and mark "Do not contact" in the tracker.

When in doubt, use a channel the business invites contact on (a "business enquiries" email, a booking or contact form) or go in person.

What to expect, honestly

I could not find trustworthy published reply rates for this kind of outreach. Anyone quoting "90% replies" is selling something. So measure your own. As a rough planning guess (my estimate, not data): if 100 well-aimed messages bring 8 to 15 replies, 3 to 6 real conversations and 1 to 3 clients, things are roughly on track. Count positive replies separately from "no thanks". If you're far below this after 50 messages, use the diagnosis table in chapter 12 before changing anything.

Each outreach day

10 new messages + the follow-ups the tracker shows as due. Log every one. About 60–90 minutes. Calls, samples and paid work come on top of this, so plan extra time on days you have a client.

Chapter 8 · As replies come

From reply to "yes"

A reply is not a sale. It's an invitation to understand what they want. The person who asks better questions wins the job.

Reply within the hour if you can

Interest fades fast. Keep notifications on during outreach weeks.

Five questions before any price

Ask these in chat or on a 10-minute call. Write the answers down: they become your brief.

  1. "What do you most want more of right now: bookings, orders, walk-ins, followers?"
  2. "Is there an offer or event coming up that we should build this around?"
  3. "Who's your best kind of customer?"
  4. "What video have you posted that did well, and one that flopped?"
  5. "Where will this run: just your page, or as a paid ad too?"

Then send a one-screen proposal

Thanks [name]. My suggestion for [business]: GOAL: more [bookings] for [offer] WHAT: 1 vertical ad (20 s) + 2 other openings to test. Captions, music. YOU SEND: 8 phone clips + logo (I'll send a shot list) TIMING: drafts 5 days after all clips arrive REVISIONS: 1 round included NOT INCLUDED: other sizes, filming PRICE: $90. 50% to start, 50% before final files. Shall I send the shot list and payment details?

Never promise sales

You control the quality and the delivery date. You don't control their product, their prices or the algorithm. Promise what you control. Clients trust you more for it.

Chapter 9 · Before any work

Deposit, agreement, getting paid

The two rules that prevent almost every beginner horror story: write it down, and get half first.

The one-page agreement

You don't need a lawyer's contract for a $100 job. You need both sides to agree, in writing, on eight things. A message they reply "agreed" to is enough for small jobs. The kit has a template.

  1. Who the two parties are.
  2. Exactly what you'll deliver: number of videos, length, sizes, versions, and what is not included.
  3. What they must send you, and that they have the right to use it (people, music, logos).
  4. Delivery date, counted from when you have all their material.
  5. Revisions: one round included, sent in one message; a set fee per extra round.
  6. Price and payment: 50% to start, 50% before final files.
  7. Rights and portfolio: they can use the finished videos once paid in full; you may show them in your portfolio only if they agree (ask, and respect a no).
  8. Cancelling: what happens to the deposit and the work done so far.

The kit has this as an editable document. It's a plain-language starting point for small jobs, not legal advice; for bigger contracts, have it checked under your local law.

Getting paid

  • Local clients: whatever they already use daily, such as bank transfer or mobile money. Ask for the transaction reference.
  • Clients abroad: options include international payment services, freelancer payout services, or a platform that handles checkout for you. Which ones work depends on your country and theirs, and the rules change. Check what is available and legal where you live before you quote a foreign client, and factor the fees into your price.
  • Deliver finals after the second payment. Send a watermarked preview for approval, then the clean files once paid.
  • Keep records of every payment and message. You may owe tax on this income. Ask someone who knows your country's rules.
Red flags: walk away
  • Refuses any deposit.
  • "Do this one cheap and there'll be lots more work."
  • Wants to overpay and have you send money back.
  • Asks you to use pirated footage or music, or to copy a competitor's ad exactly.
  • Is rude before paying. It gets worse after.

Chapter 10 · Each job

Deliver so they come back

A repeat client costs you zero outreach. Good delivery is your cheapest marketing.

The shot list you send them

Please film these on your phone, upright (vertical), 5–8 seconds each, in good light. Don't worry about sound. 1. The front of the business from outside 2. Wide shot of the inside 3. Your product/service close up, in action (3 clips) 4. A happy customer or the finished result 5. You or your team, smiling at camera 6. Anything that makes you different Also send: your logo, your offer in one sentence, and 1–2 customer reviews I can quote.

Your workflow

  1. Day 0: deposit in, shot list sent, delivery date confirmed in writing.
  2. Material arrives: check it the same day. Ask for re-shoots now, not later.
  3. Script first: six lines of on-screen text. Send it for a quick OK before you edit. This avoids most revisions.
  4. Edit using the 8-part structure from chapter 3.
  5. Preview: send watermarked drafts one day early.
  6. One revision round: ask for all changes in one message.
  7. Final payment, then finals: the clean files you agreed in the proposal, named clearly.
  8. One week later: ask how it performed.

Small things clients remember

  • You deliver a day early.
  • File names make sense: BusinessName_Offer_v1_9x16.mp4.
  • Optional, if you have time: a suggested post caption. (Small extras are nice; just don't let them become expected scope.)
  • You tell them the best version to post first, and why.
  • You never go quiet. If you're late, you say so before the deadline.
When revisions don't stop

"Happy to make those. That's beyond the round we agreed, so it would be $15 for this extra round. Shall I go ahead?" Calm, friendly, in writing. The revision request form in the kit helps clients send all changes at once.

Chapter 11 · Day 25–30

Turn one client into three

Your first happy client is worth more than the fee. They are proof, a referral source and possibly a monthly customer.

1. Ask for a testimonial the moment they're pleased

So glad you like them! Could you write 2–3 sentences I can share: what you needed, what it was like working with me, and what you think of the result? A short voice note works too and I'll type it up for your OK.

Use their words exactly, with their name and business, and only with permission. Never write a testimonial yourself or tidy it into something they didn't say.

2. Ask for one introduction

One more thing: do you know one other business owner who could use videos like these? If you introduce us and they book, your next video is 20% off.

3. Offer the monthly plan when results show

The offer ad got [their number] views, nice. If you want to post regularly, I can do 8 short videos a month from one batch of footage for $320, two a week. Want to try one month?

Only quote numbers they gave you or that you can both see on the post. Run the monthly batch through the pricing calculator first. Some clients only need one campaign, and that's fine: no repeat doesn't mean you did badly.

4. Put the proof to work

  • Add the real project to the top of your portfolio, above the spec pieces.
  • Post a before/after of the job (with permission).
  • Add one line to your outreach: "I recently made [X] for [business], here's the result: [link]." In my experience, a real client name makes people take a message more seriously.
  • Go back to everyone who said "not now" with the new work.

5. Review your price

After three paid jobs, the launch price ends, as you said it would. Check your real hours against the calculator, and set your next price from that.

If day 30 comes and you have zero clients

That happens, and it isn't a verdict on you. You now have a portfolio, an offer, a list and 100 messages of data, which is more than most editors ever build. Change one thing (the lane, the first line, or the price) and run two more weeks. One change at a time, so you know what worked.
