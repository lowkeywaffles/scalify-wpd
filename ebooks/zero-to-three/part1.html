What this book is, and what it isn't

You can edit. Nobody is paying you yet, or only a friend has. This book is a 30-day plan to change that, by selling one specific thing to one specific kind of buyer: short promo and ad videos for small businesses.

It is a field guide, so it is built to be used, not just read. Every chapter ends with a task. The scripts are written out word for word. The kit that comes with it has a tracker, the scripts as a text file, a client brief and a simple agreement you can adapt.

Honest limits

This book cannot promise you clients or income. Nobody can. What it gives you is a clear method, a daily workload, and a way to measure what is happening so you can fix the right thing.

The numbers in it (prices, reply rates) are ranges from public sources and my own experience, and yours will differ. The agreement template is a plain-language starting point, not legal advice. Tax and payment rules depend on your country, so check yours.

The whole method in six lines
  1. Choose one type of business.
  2. Make three sample ads for businesses like them.
  3. Offer one clear package at one clear price.
  4. Contact 5 to 10 of those businesses every day, with a message about their business.
  5. Take a deposit, deliver fast, ask for a testimonial.
  6. Use that testimonial to win the next two.

Chapter 1 · Day 1

Why good editors stay at zero

Almost nobody fails at this because their edits are bad. They fail for five boring reasons, and every one of them can be fixed this month.

1. They sell "video editing"

A bakery owner does not wake up wanting video editing. She wants more people to order cakes this weekend. "I'm a video editor, do you need any editing?" asks her to work out what you could do for her. "I make 20-second ads for bakeries that show the cake being made, sized for Instagram and TikTok" does that work for her.

2. Their portfolio shows the wrong thing

Anime edits, gaming montages and velocity edits prove you know the software. They don't prove you can sell a haircut. A business owner needs to see something that looks like what she would get.

3. They wait to be found

Posting a profile on a marketplace and waiting puts you in a queue with thousands of editors, many with reviews you don't have yet. Beginners report very low reply rates there. One estimate from a freelancer mentor is about 1 reply in 15 for specific proposals and 1 in 100 for copy-paste ones. Direct contact with businesses you chose is slower per message and much better per reply.

4. They send ten messages and stop

Outreach is a numbers game with a quality rule. Ten messages tells you nothing. A hundred good ones tells you a lot. This plan has you send about 100 over two and a half weeks.

5. They don't know what to say when someone answers "how much?"

So they panic, say a tiny number, or disappear. Chapter 4 fixes the price. Chapter 8 gives you the words.

Why businesses, not YouTubers

Most beginner advice says to chase YouTubers. That works for some people, and it is crowded. Small businesses are a different pond:

  • A video that brings in customers pays for itself, so price is easier to justify.
  • Most small businesses post weak video or none, and they know it.
  • There are far more small businesses than full-time creators, in every city and online.
  • Three samples are enough to start. You don't need to match a famous creator's style.
Task for today

Write down, honestly, which of the five reasons is yours. Most people have two. You'll check back on day 30.

Chapter 2 · Day 1–2

Pick a lane

For the next 30 days you make promo videos for one type of business. Not forever. For 30 days. A lane makes every later step easier: your samples match, your message is specific, and one happy client can refer you to others like them.

What a good lane looks like

  • They already post, badly. A business with an active Instagram or TikTok full of blurry clips has already decided video matters. You're not convincing them, you're upgrading them.
  • One new customer is worth real money. A dentist, gym, salon, tutor or real-estate agent earns a lot more from one new customer than your video costs.
  • The product looks good on camera. Food, hair, fitness, cars, interiors, events, fashion.
  • You can reach the owner. The person who reads the DMs is the person who decides.
  • You understand it a little. If you lift weights, gyms are easier for you than law firms.

Where the footage comes from

You're an editor, not a camera crew. There are three honest ways to work:

  1. They film on a phone, you direct. You send a shot list (chapter 10 has one). Most owners can film eight 5-second clips.
  2. You use what they already posted. Their existing clips, photos and reviews, re-cut properly.
  3. No footage at all. Text, photos, screen recordings and motion graphics. Ideal for apps, online stores and services.

Local clients can also be filmed by you on your phone. That's a bonus, not a requirement.

Chapter 3 · Day 3–7

Build a 3-piece portfolio with no clients

You don't need permission to make a sample. You need three short videos that look like what your lane would buy. These are called spec pieces, and every agency started with them.

The three pieces

PieceWhat it isLength
1. The offer adOne clear offer: "First class free", "20% off this week", "Order by Friday".15–20 s
2. The "why us" adThree reasons to choose this business, shown not told.20–30 s
3. The before/afterA real weak video from a business in your lane, re-edited by you, shown side by side.15–25 s

Piece 3 does the heaviest lifting. It shows the gap between what they have and what you make in a few seconds, with no explaining.

Where to get material, legally

  • Free stock footage with a licence that allows commercial use (for example Pexels, Pixabay and Mixkit). Read each site's licence page before you use a clip.
  • Your own phone. Film your own gym session, a coffee being made, a friend's shop with their OK.
  • An invented brand. Make up "Northside Barbers". An invented brand has no owner to complain.
  • Licensed music and sounds only. Use libraries that allow commercial use. Chart music can get a client's ad muted or taken down.
Be honest about spec work

Label samples as "concept" or "spec ad". Never imply a business hired you when it didn't, and don't use a real company's logo as if it were a client. For the before/after, either use a business that agreed, or blur the name and say "re-edit of a real post, shown for comparison". It protects you, and owners respect it.

The 8-part ad that works for almost any business

  1. Hook (0–2 s). The most striking shot or a short line: "Your Friday plans, sorted."
  2. Problem or desire (2–5 s). What the customer wants.
  3. The product in action (5–12 s). Fast cuts, 1 to 1.5 seconds each.
  4. Proof (12–16 s). A review quote, a number, a before/after.
  5. Offer (16–19 s). One offer, one line.
  6. Call to action (last 2 s). "Book in bio", "Order on WhatsApp", with the handle on screen.
  7. Captions throughout. Many people watch on mute.
  8. Sound. A music bed and 4 to 8 sound effects on the cuts.

Quality bar before you show anyone

  • Vertical 9:16, 1080×1920, sharp.
  • Text is inside the safe area (not under the app's buttons or caption).
  • Readable on a phone at arm's length. Nothing smaller than a caption.
  • Something changes on screen at least every 2 seconds.
  • The offer and how to act on it are clear with the sound off.
  • No spelling mistakes. Read every word aloud.
  • Shown to one person outside editing who can say what's being sold after one watch.

Where your portfolio lives

Keep it simple: one link that opens fast on a phone. A pinned post or highlight on an Instagram or TikTok account used only for your work is enough. A free one-page site or a shared folder also works. Put the before/after first, then the other two, then one line on what you do and how to reach you.

Task (5 days)

Day 3: gather material and write three 6-line scripts. Days 4–6: one piece per day. Day 7: fix, export, set up the one link, and get one outside opinion.

Chapter 4 · Day 8

Your offer and your prices

An offer is a sentence a busy owner can say yes to. It names what they get, how fast, and for how much.

Write your offer sentence

I make [number] short promo videos for [type of business] that [result they want], delivered in [days] days, for [price].

Example: "I make 3 short promo videos for gyms that show off your space and your offer, delivered in 5 days, for $150."

Three packages

Offer three choices. Most people pick the middle one, and three options change the question from "yes or no?" to "which one?".

Ranges reflect public 2026 figures for short-form work (commonly $20–$75 per clip from freelancers, more with motion graphics) and my own jobs at $50–$200. Local prices vary a lot: adjust to what businesses near you can pay.

Find your own floor price

Never guess. Work it out once:

Hours for one ad (be honest, include revisions) = ___ Minimum you accept per hour = ___ Floor price = hours × hourly minimum = ___ Add 20% for messages, exports, fixes = ___ ← never go below this

If a starter ad takes you 4 hours and your minimum is $10 an hour, your floor is $40 and your price with the buffer is $48. Round to $50.

Rules that protect you

  • First three clients: a launch price, said out loud. "My normal price is $150. I'm taking three launch clients at $100 in exchange for a testimonial." A discount with a reason and a limit is honest. A fake "normal price" is not, so only say it if you will really charge it next.
  • Never free for a full project. A short free sample to open a conversation is fine (chapter 7). A whole job for "exposure" is not.
  • One revision round is included. Extra rounds cost a set fee. Say it before you start.
  • 50% before you start. Always. Chapter 9.
  • Raise your price after every third client by 20 to 30 percent until people start saying no about a third of the time.
